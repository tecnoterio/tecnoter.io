People ask what the point of a long career is, on the reasonable grounds that
everything I recommend was documented before they were born.

It is not knowledge retention. Almost nothing specific has survived. The
vendors I started with are gone or acquired. Several languages I was expert
in are now museum pieces. One filesystem I was very good at is genuinely
obsolete and I am not going to name it.

What survived is a shape of mind, and it is mostly about knowing what to be
suspicious of.

I know which parts of a system are load-bearing and which are decoration,
and that is a judgement rather than a fact. On a system nobody has touched in
two years, the strange and elaborate thing near the middle of the file is
load-bearing and the tidy thing at the top is decoration, and it is
amazingly hard for a new person to see that.

I have watched a field of best practices arrive, get adopted everywhere,
and get partly reversed a decade later, and I recognised the shape of it
early enough to notice the current one doing the same thing. Not because I
knew better. Because I had seen the previous version fail, and the previous
version had seemed unassailable.

I know the failure modes that are not in the runbook. The things that only
happen on a Friday, or only after an upgrade, or only on the one host in the
rack nobody has touched. That knowledge dies with the people who have it,
which is an argument for writing it down that I only started taking
seriously about fifteen years ago.

And I have made every mistake that is worth making once. Not cheaply, and
not without consequences, but the number of times you can be wrong in a
career is smaller than people think, and I have spent most of mine.

The part nobody asks about: it also makes me slower, and I think that is
usually correct. When something is new, I do not reach for it first. I wait
until I understand why the thing it replaces was built the way it was,
because that is where the actual information is and it is rarely in the
announcement.
