A second roundup, this one about where the writing actually is. Retro
computing has good documentation and mediocre news coverage, and knowing the
difference saves a lot of time.

**Serious technical work.** [Pagetable.com](https://www.pagetable.com/) does
C64 and 128 hardware reverse engineering with disassembly and scanned
original manuals, and it is close to the equivalent of reading a kernel
source. [Leaded Solder](https://www.leadedsolder.com/) covers repair,
restoration and bare-metal programming across Japanese and British machines.
The [MiSTer wiki](https://github.com/MiSTer-devel/Wiki_MiSTer/wiki) is the
best-written FPGA recreation documentation available, and its
[why FPGA](https://github.com/MiSTer-devel/Wiki_MiSTer/wiki/Why-FPGA) page
is a genuinely good explanation of the emulation versus replication
distinction. [The Retro Hacker](https://www.theretrohacker.com/) is an FPGA
MSX2 clone and a RISC-V console, which is the kind of thing that should be
on an engineer's desk.

**Forums with actual engineers in them.** [VOGONS](https://www.vogons.org/)
for retro PC hardware and DOS, which is the most technically serious of the
Anglo-American forums. [Retro Computing Forum](https://retrocomputingforum.com/)
for minicomputers and micros. [Atari.org](https://forums.atari.org/) is still
the definitive Atari resource. [MSX](https://www.msx.org/) is the central hub
for MSX and has one of the better-maintained wikis in the hobby.
[Spectrum Computing](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/) is powered by a
database, which means its information is structured rather than threaded.

**Long-running personal blogs, which are consistently better than the
magazines.** [Byte Cellar](https://bytecellar.com/) has been writing about
vintage computing for years and covers conferences properly.
[Vintage Computing and Gaming](https://www.vintagecomputing.com/) is a
blogazine with the same longevity. [Bits By 8](https://www.bitsby8.com/) does
hardware deep dives. [The Retro Hacker](https://www.theretrohacker.com/) is
an FPGA MSX2 clone and a RISC-V console, which is the kind of thing that
should be on an engineer's desk.

**Databases, if you want to know what exists.** [Bitsavers](https://bitsavers.org/)
for documentation, close to two terabytes of manuals, schematics and
software. [ZXDB](https://spectrumcomputing.co.uk/) via Spectrum Computing,
which is the best-organised retro game database in existence.
[Gury's Atari 8-bit Forever](https://gury.atari8.info/) is a similar
effort for Atari.

**Events, if you are near one.** [VCF](https://vcfed.org/) runs regional
gatherings and is where the machines get switched on rather than discussed.
[Vintage Computer Festival](https://www.vintagecomputing.com/) covers the
same ground in blog form.

## A note on tone

The retro computing community is unusually good at documenting what it does,
and unusually bad at hedging. People reverse engineer a chip for a year and
write it up in full. That habit is worth something professionally, and it is
in noticeable contrast to how much of the infrastructure industry publishes.

If you are looking for the anti-pattern to avoid, it is not in these
communities. It is in vendor blogs about a category the vendor invented.
